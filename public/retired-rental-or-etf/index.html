Your case · retired, with a flat to rent

Retired with a flat to rent? A steady income, without tenants.

Tenants, repairs, the building’s bills, months with no rent. If what you want is extra money every month next to your pension, and your money there when you need it, here’s the other way: sell, and put it into a calm mix of ETFs.

Sound familiar?

  • The tenant stops paying. Getting them out can take many months, sometimes more than a year, with lawyers and courts. Meanwhile, you pay the bills.
  • They leave out of the blue, and the flat sits empty for months. Property tax, building costs and bills keep coming.
  • Life changes: a couple splits up, one leaves and the other can’t pay any more. Kids arrive, and asking a family to leave gets even harder.
  • Something always breaks: the boiler, the roof, a leak, an appliance. And they call you.
  • The building: extra charges, meetings, works decided by others that you pay for too.
  • Taxes: tax on the rent, property tax, and the tax return every year.
  • You need money, but you can’t sell: with a tenant inside and a long contract, you either sell for less or wait years.
  • The rules are on the tenant’s side: you have few tools and long waits.

In the end you’re a bit of a hostage in your own flat. It doesn’t have to be that way: below you can see the other way, with your numbers.

Why not everything in shares

  • When you’re retired, you’re taking money out. If markets fall just as you start, you sell low and the money lasts less.
  • Bonds are the brakes: loans to governments that pay interest. They move less, so in a bad year you can live on them while shares recover.
  • Fewer swings, less stress: a calmer mix earns less, but it lets you sleep. At 70 that counts more than one extra percent.

How it works in practice

  1. Sell the flat. If there’s a tenant with a contract, you either sell with the tenant in (for less) or wait until the contract ends.
  2. Keep 1–2 years of withdrawals in a savings deposit.
  3. Put the rest into a mixed ETF (for example 40% shares and 60% bonds, it rebalances by itself), or into two ETFs: one world shares, one euro government bonds.
  4. Once or twice a year, sell a piece of the ETF and top up the deposit. Every month, move your “second pension” to your current account.

It’s not only about money

  • No more tenants: no calls about the boiler, no chasing late rent, no building meetings.
  • Your money, when you want it: for health, to help your children, for a trip.
  • Easier for heirs: ETFs split in a minute; a flat often means arguments.
  • But: if that flat means a lot to you, that’s a fair reason to keep it.

Taxes to check in your country

Selling the flat may be taxed on the gain; what you take out of ETFs is usually taxed only on the gain part. Rules depend on your country, and sometimes there are breaks for retirees. Ask an accountant before selling.

Questions

What are bonds, in plain words?
Loans to governments or companies that pay you interest. In a normal year they move little, and they soften the falls of shares. But they’re not zero risk: in 2022 they fell too.
What if the money runs out before I do?
The calculator above tells you how long it lasts with your numbers. Studies of the past say that taking 3–4% a year from a mixed portfolio lasted 25–30 years in most cases. It’s not a guarantee: if you want to be calmer, take out a bit less.
One ETF or two?
One “all-in-one” mixed ETF (for example 40% shares and 60% bonds) does everything by itself, including keeping the mix. Two ETFs, one shares and one bonds, cost a little less but you rebalance yourself. For most people one is simpler.
Isn’t a house safer?
A house doesn’t show a price every day, so it feels calmer. But it can lose value too, it can stay empty, and you can’t sell a piece of it when you need money. With ETFs you see every swing, but your money is spread over thousands of companies and bonds, and it’s there when you need it.
